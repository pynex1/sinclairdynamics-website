Where I Am Now

The Solar Monitor

The Solar Monitor watches six installations for several owners, and each one reports through a different manufacturer's platform. Those platforms do not agree with one another about what they are measuring or how often. Reconciling that is half the work, and it is the half nobody finds interesting.

The other half is that nobody reads dashboards. Numbers and charts are placed in front of a person who has no time to interpret them, so the interpretation never happens. This platform observes each site on a schedule instead, writes up what changed and what is likely causing it, and answers questions about it in plain language. The charts still exist. They are not the point.

The monitor can be talked to. A site owner asks what happened yesterday and gets a sentence rather than a chart. The assistant on the contact page of this site is the same build answering a different subject. Neither one improvises: what each is allowed to see is settled before it is asked anything.

How I Got Here

The work was never
in one discipline.

The route runs from national infrastructure to a software platform. What the stops have in common is not a subject. Each one sat where two things met, and no single specialist owned the ground in between.

Consulting Engineering

Electrical inside civil

I started in a large consulting firm on national roadways, toll facilities, urban precincts, and the electrical systems that run underneath them. Electrical infrastructure does not sit beside civil infrastructure. It runs through it, and the drawings are produced by different people. The Johannesburg infrastructure upgrades for the 2010 FIFA World Cup were where I first saw what happens to a programme when the date cannot move.

West Africa

Design meets what was built

I worked on the commissioning and acceptance testing of a private toll concession in West Africa, the first of its kind in the region. Commissioning is a seam by definition. It is the point where the thing that was designed meets the thing that was actually built, and the two are never identical. Doing it under an unfamiliar regulatory framework adds a second seam that appears on no drawing.

Electrical Contracting

Engineering meets money

I moved into project and contract management, and eventually ran a division of a national contractor: several project managers, multiple active contracts, tendering strategy, subcontractor development. A technically correct decision that loses the contract is not a correct decision. Where exactly that line falls is not taught anywhere, and getting it wrong is expensive in both directions.

Agri-processing

A plant, investors, four countries

I co-founded an agri-processing venture and led its operations, working between South Africa, Malawi and Uganda, with investors in Cyprus and the United States. Cross-border logistics, commercial and legal negotiation, and underneath all of it a plant that had to work. It did not go to plan. I learned more there about what a business actually needs from its engineering than on any project that went well.

The Platform

Hardware meets software

Sinclair Dynamics is the practice I run now, and the monitoring platform came out of it. It is the same kind of edge as the others: a physical system on one side, software watching it on the other, and the decisions that matter all in between. The platform stands on established engineering, on mathematics settled since the 1940s, and on a capability that did not exist two years ago. None of those three is mine. Putting them together is.
